# Part 2: Published research on AI review panels, compared with the Agent Referee log

> Kept as it was independently checked. Its two halves came back needs_changes, and [Part 3](part-3-corrected.md) corrects it. In particular, its analysis of the split verdicts rests on a wrong fail-vote count, and the "one real issue per nine false ones" ratio is not in its source.

*This is Part 2, the web-research companion to [Part 1](part-1-log.md) (Claude's written log of the Oct 27, 2026 review-3 receipts). Part 1 records what happened in the 18 paid checks; this part asks what the published literature from 2023 to October 2026 would have predicted and how well the log fits it.*

Published research supports the core design of review-3: independent reviews from models of different companies, combined by majority vote. That is the configuration the evidence backs best, and in this log the panel caught real defects of exactly the kinds the literature says AI reviewers catch well. They were a logic bug visible in the changed lines (operator precedence) and sequencing gaps stated in a plan (paid routes exposed before alerts were proven, an unproven phone alert path). The research is equally clear about the limits, and the log hit every one of them. **Three models are not three independent checks**: frontier models share blind spots, and one 2026 preprint found a nine-judge panel worth about two independent votes. Single judges are noisy, lenient when unsure (more so for smaller models), and swayed by how the request is framed. Repeated review rounds produce more false findings than real ones. Runtime and load properties are out of reach for any reviewer reading text. The log's one significant miss shows that last limit at work: a per-IP limit the reviewers prompted, applied as written, held one address to 23 parallel reviews, and only a real-money load test exposed it. The research also shows the panel was weaker than its three seats suggest. Gemini 3.7 Flash passed 16 of 17 items and raised one issue in total. The author wrote every brief. One reviewer shared the author's model family. Five checks graded a stale file. The evidence-backed fixes are mostly procedural and cheap: pin the artifact, neutralise the brief, change how the lenient seat counts, stop rerunning, verify each finding, and send anything about limits or load to execution, not to a vote.

## Single judges are noisy and lenient, and the brief steers them

The widely quoted claim that GPT-4 agrees with humans as often as humans agree with each other (about 85% against 81%) is true only for chat preferences with ties excluded. With ties counted, agreement fell to 66% ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)). Across 20 datasets and over 70,000 items, **average Cohen's kappa against human labels was only 0.28 for GPT-4o** and 0.22 for Gemini-1.5, and every model tracked non-expert annotators more closely than experts ([Bavaresco et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18403v3)). On pairs where one answer was objectively correct, GPT-4o scored 50.86%, "no better than random guessing," while the reasoning model o3-mini reached 80.86%. The same benchmark found **coding harder to verify than to solve** ([Tan et al., JudgeBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.12784)). Pass/fail review of plans, readiness reports and code sits much closer to this hard, expert end than to chat preference.

Leniency is the dominant direction of error. On reference-graded QA, the chance that a judge marked a wrong answer "correct" was 0.69 for GPT-4 and 0.82 to 0.99 for open models from 7B to 70B. Judges caught wholly wrong answers 97-98% of the time but **under-specified or partly correct answers only 23-34% of the time** ([Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624v5)). Plans and readiness reports are almost always "partly correct" artifacts. That makes Gemini 3.7 Flash's record (16 passes in 17 votes, one issue raised) the pattern this research predicts for a fast-tier reviewer. The log cannot prove Flash was wrong on any single item. But on items where Claude Opus and GPT-6 Luna found defects that were later confirmed, such as the precedence bug and the non-simultaneous load test, a near-automatic pass contributes almost no detection.

The judge's instructions matter as much as the judge. Changing only the prompt wording moved GPT-4's kappa with humans from 0.518 to 0.725 on the same data ([Verga et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18796v2)). Adding one sentence about consequences made judges more lenient in **58 of 72 conditions**, and a reasoning judge's chain of thought mentioned that framing in 0 of 4,560 judgments ([Gupta et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15224)). Judges also give way to persuasive reasoning even when it reaches a wrong conclusion ([Kim and Khashabi](https://preview.aclanthology.org/new-sigs/2025.findings-emnlp.1222/)). In one study, a classifier given only the rubric text predicted a small judge's verdicts with 0.83-0.90 accuracy, without reading the response at all ([Bagaria et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.02942)). No study tests the exact Agent Referee case, where the author of the work writes the brief. Every part of that risk is documented, though, and the bias would not show in the reviewers' reasoning. That makes "the author wrote every brief" the log's most important unmeasured weakness.

Self-preference is real but did not show up as leniency here. GPT-4 recognised its own summaries about 73.5% of the time, and self-recognition tracked self-preference ([Panickssery et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076v1)). Preference leakage between related models averaged 8.9% within the same family and series ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.01534v3)). Excluding the judge's own model does not remove affinity bias ([Goel et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04313v2)). In the log, though, Claude Opus 5.5 was the harshest reviewer: 15 of 18 needs_changes and **144 issues, about eight per check**. The honest reading is that the same-family risk was not leniency on verdicts. Instead, it may sit in what Opus and the author both fail to see, and a vote count cannot reveal that.

## A three-model panel buys bias control more than independent votes

The founding panel study matched three small models from three companies against a single GPT-4 judge. The panel won on kappa (NQ 0.763 against 0.627), showed the least bias spread, and cost seven to eight times less ([Verga et al., PoLL](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18796v2)). Simple majority voting explains most of the gains credited to multi-agent debate ([Choi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)). In a review-specific study, a 2-of-3 majority over independent reviews gave the best F1 of any condition, though only in a lightly validated appendix ([Song](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16244v1)). Review-3's independent, parallel, majority-vote design is therefore the right baseline.

The case against counting three seats as three checks is also strong. Across hundreds of models, when two models were both wrong they picked **the same wrong answer about 60% of the time against 33% by chance**, and more accurate models were more correlated with each other ([Kim et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07962v1)). Judge scores track judge-model similarity (mean r = 0.84), and capable models' mistakes are converging ([Goel et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04313v2)). A May 2026 preprint found nine frontier judges from seven families worth about **two effective votes**. The best single judge matched or beat the full panel in every condition, and smarter aggregation closed at most 11% of the gap ([Kohli, via Hugging Face Papers](https://huggingface.co/papers/2605.29800)). That preprint is single-author, unreplicated, and does not name its models on the pages read, so it should be weighted lightly. It agrees, however, with Amazon's ICML 2026 work, which warns that agreement "may be independent evidence or a shared blind spot." In that work, dependence-aware aggregation beat majority voting only once there was enough labelled data ([Amazon Science](https://amazon.science/blog/when-llm-judges-agree-should-we-believe-them)).

Applied to the log, these findings expose a structural problem the receipts alone do not. Gemini Flash votes pass almost every time, so a panel **pass needs only one more pass, from either Claude Opus or GPT-6 Luna**. In effect the pass bar is 1-of-2 strict reviewers, not 2-of-3. A needs_changes verdict needs both strict reviewers to agree. A split (pass, needs_changes and fail all present) appears whenever one strict reviewer escalates to fail. GPT-6 Luna supplied 5 fail votes, and Gemini Flash's single fail accounts for the rest of the default panel's fails. Splits in this log were therefore mostly disagreements between Opus and GPT about severity, with Flash's pass as background. The 6-of-18 split rate (33%) is a stricter kind of disagreement than a 2-1 vote, because there is no majority at all. It still fits with kappa values of 0.28 to 0.51 between judges in the literature ([Bavaresco et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18403v3); [Yagubyan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13685)). No published source gives a base rate for three-company panels on real review work, so this comparison is loose.

## Panels catch what is written down and miss what only happens at runtime

On real pull requests, AI reviewers have low recall and low precision. The best overall F1 on 1,000 verified PRs was 19.38% ([SWR-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01494v1)). On another benchmark, no model found more than 31% of human-flagged issues, and issues that lived in dependent files had near-zero detection ([SWE-PRBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26130v1), single-author and LLM-scored). Production figures look better, at about 75% of comments rated useful at Uber and 75% precision at ByteDance. Those figures measure precision after heavy filtering, not recall of real defects ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/ug/en/blog/ureview); [BitsAI-CR](https://conf.researchr.org/details/fse-2025/fse-2025-industry-papers/24/BitsAI-CR-Automated-Code-Review-via-LLM-in-Practice)). In an unfiltered field trial at Mozilla and Ubisoft, only 7-8% of comments were accepted ([RevMate](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07091v1)). LLM detection of performance bugs from source code is a published negative result ([SANER 2026](https://conf.researchr.org/details/saner-2026/saner-2026-reproducibility-studies-and-negative-results-rene-track/2/An-Empirical-Investigation-on-the-use-of-Large-Language-Models-for-Performance-Bug-De)).

The log's catches and its miss fall on either side of that line. The precedence bug was in the changed lines. The deploy-order and alert-path problems were contradictions between stated steps in a plan. Both are text-checkable. The 23-per-IP limit was a property of real traffic, where many legitimate agents can share one egress address, and no reviewer reading a diff can see that. The miss also has a well-documented cause. GPT-4o's false-rejection rate on correct code rose from 26.2% to 73.2% as prompts asked for explanations and fixes. When the original and the LLM-"fixed" code were both run against tests, the "over-repair" filter cut false rejections from 54.8% to 16.3% ([Jin and Chen](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00539v1)). Counting in-flight reviews against a failure allowance was a hardening suggestion judged against an imagined threat. The live load test played the role of that test filter. Practitioners in the Beko field study warned that applying bot changes without care can "introduce serious bugs" ([Beko study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.18531)). Microsoft's reviewer never auto-commits, and a human must apply each change ([Microsoft](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/engineering-at-microsoft/enhancing-code-quality-at-scale-with-ai-powered-code-reviews/)).

The load-test episode also shows when a persistent "fail" is worth heeding. GPT-6 Luna kept failing the readiness report until a six-machine test sent 3,000 requests within 0.13 seconds. That was not false-positive pressure. It was a demand for evidence, and evidence closed it. The judging literature points the same way: reference-guided grading cut GPT-4's math-grading failures from 70% to 15% ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)). Reviewers judge best when grading evidence against a stated criterion, and worst when grading prose against their own priors.

## Rerunning the same report stops paying after a few rounds

The readiness report went through nine drafts, and returns fell off after about the sixth run. The only study of repeated review rounds on code and documents predicts this. Using Claude Opus 4.6 on 30 artifacts with injected errors, a single fresh review had the best F1 (0.376). A second round lifted recall from 0.529 to 0.607, but false positives rose from 5.2 to 8.5 per artifact. That works out to **about one new real issue per nine new false ones**, and the single review won in 25 of 30 artifacts ([Song](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16244v1)). The author names two failure modes, "false positive pressure" and "review target drift." The study is single-author, uses one model and injected errors, and has no human baseline, so its numbers should not be carried over directly. Its direction agrees with the debate literature, which finds that unsteered extra rounds do not improve expected correctness ([Choi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)). Opus's 144 issues are where this matters most. Without checking which issues were real, the log cannot say how many drafts were driven by real defects and how many by reviewers asked once more to find something.

Run-to-run noise is the opposite risk. One weak source found a single judge flipping 13.6% of verdicts between identical runs, with hard items needing about 15 trials to stabilise ([Yagubyan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13685)). A rerun of an unchanged artifact therefore mostly samples noise. Reruns should follow a real change, and should stop when new findings stop being verified as real.

## Weak sources and real gaps

Several findings above come from single-author preprints that have not been replicated. These are Yagubyan (mini models, 29 tasks), Kohli (models unnamed in the pages read, no peer-reviewed venue), Song (one model, injected errors) and Kumar's SWE-PRBench (scored by an LLM judge). Bagaria et al. tested one 7B judge in a medical domain, and neither it nor Gupta et al. states a date on its PDF. The stronger anchors are peer-reviewed or industry sources: Zheng et al., Thakur et al., JudgeBench, CALM, PoLL, Kim et al. and Goel et al. (ICML 2025), Li et al. (ICLR 2026), the Amazon ICML 2026 work, and the Uber, Microsoft, ByteDance and Beko deployments. Most of their measurements, however, use models from 2023 to 2025.

The gaps matter for Agent Referee specifically. No published study measures any of the log's models (Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Luna, Gemini 3.7 Flash, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Gemini 4 Argon) as reviewers, or the error correlation among them. No rigorous study covers AI review of deployment or readiness plans. Nothing tests the effect of an author-written brief against a neutral one. Nothing reports split rates or ground-truth accuracy for three-company panels on real work. Nothing measures in production how often applied AI suggestions cause regressions. The literature is also silent on a pattern in this log: Claude proposed 9 of the 18 checks on its own initiative, against 5 requested by the owner and 4 carried over from an earlier session. When the author chooses when to be reviewed, timing and selection can affect results, and nothing published measures that. Finally, a sample of 18 checks is far too small to estimate reviewer weights or dependencies ([Amazon Science](https://amazon.science/blog/when-llm-judges-agree-should-we-believe-them)).

## Recommendations for Agent Referee and its users

| Recommendation | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|
| **Pin the exact artifact.** Put a content hash in the brief and on the receipt, and refuse the review before payment if the submitted text does not match the hash the author declares. | Five of 18 checks reviewed a stale copy of the report. This is a process fix with no literature behind it; none is needed. |
| **Neutralise the brief.** Use a fixed Agent Referee template with acceptance criteria only, no description of the work's quality, no stakes or deployment language, and no argued case. Users should state what "pass" means as checkable claims. | Stakes framing shifted verdicts in 58 of 72 conditions, invisibly ([Gupta et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15224)); persuasive reasoning sways judges ([Kim and Khashabi](https://preview.aclanthology.org/new-sigs/2025.findings-emnlp.1222/)); rubric text alone predicts verdicts ([Bagaria et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.02942)). |
| **Attach evidence and grade against it.** Send test output, load-test logs and reference answers with the artifact, and ask reviewers to judge whether the evidence supports each claim. | Reference-guided grading cut failures from 70% to 15% ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)). GPT-6 Luna's fail was settled by a 3,000-request test, not by rewording. |
| **Fix the lenient seat.** Replace Gemini 3.7 Flash with a stronger reviewer from a third company (Gemini 4 Argon or DeepSeek V4 Pro were used once), or require that a pass include at least one strict reviewer's pass and no fail. | Smaller judges are lenient when in doubt ([Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624v5)); reasoning judges verify much better ([JudgeBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.12784)). The alternative pass rule is untested and should be trialled against outcomes. |
| **Disclose family overlap.** When the author's model family matches a reviewer, say so on the receipt. Keep that reviewer's findings, but do not let its vote be the deciding pass. | Same-family leakage is documented ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.01534v3)); excluding the judge's own model does not remove affinity bias, so similarity should be reported ([Goel et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04313v2)). |
| **Treat a split as a work order, not a verdict.** Turn the dissenting reviewer's main claim into a concrete question, settle it with a test or a human check, and do not rerun to break the tie. | Agreement and disagreement can both be shared blind spots or noise ([Amazon Science](https://amazon.science/blog/when-llm-judges-agree-should-we-believe-them)); reruns mostly resample noise ([Yagubyan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13685), weak source). |
| **Verify findings before revising, and cap reruns.** Triage each issue as real or not before the next draft. Rerun only after a real change, and stop when a round yields no verified new issue (the log suggests about six rounds at most; the literature suggests fewer). | Later rounds produce about nine false findings per real one ([Song](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16244v1), weak source); debate without new signal does not improve correctness ([Choi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17536v2)). |
| **Send limits, capacity and access policy to execution.** Any fix, especially one a reviewer suggested, that changes rate limits, concurrency, allowances or routing must pass a load test with realistic clients (including many agents behind one IP) before it ships. A panel pass is not evidence here. | Runtime and latent issues have near-zero detection ([SWE-PRBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26130v1)); reviewer fixes overcorrect and tests catch it ([Jin and Chen](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00539v1)). The 23-per-IP limit was found only this way. |
| **Check length before payment.** Split long documents into sections under about 5,000 characters, or refuse before charging, as already happened with Claude Haiku on the 12,000-character report. | Consistency-check recall fell from 0.89 to 0.47 as documents grew past 5,000 characters ([Fujitsu](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09975v1)). |
| **Keep a human on apply, and log who asked for each check.** No review suggestion should be applied automatically. Each receipt should record whether the check was requested by the owner, proposed by the author, or carried over from an earlier session. | Microsoft requires explicit human application of every change ([Microsoft](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/engineering-at-microsoft/enhancing-code-quality-at-scale-with-ai-powered-code-reviews/)). The selection effect of author-initiated checks is unstudied. |
| **Label outcomes to calibrate later.** For each issue, record whether it was real, and for each verdict, whether later evidence confirmed it. Use this to measure each reviewer's precision, and only later to weight votes. | Dependence-aware aggregation beats majority vote only with enough data ([Amazon Science](https://amazon.science/blog/when-llm-judges-agree-should-we-believe-them)); production teams track adoption to tune reviewers ([BitsAI-CR](https://conf.researchr.org/details/fse-2025/fse-2025-industry-papers/24/BitsAI-CR-Automated-Code-Review-via-LLM-in-Practice)). |

## Conclusion

The main lesson of setting the log beside the literature is that review-3's value lies in its issues, not its verdicts. At $0.20 a check, the panel produced the precedence bug, the deploy-order catch and the evidence demand that forced a real simultaneous load test, all of them cheap finds against costly failures. The pass, needs_changes and split labels carry much less information than they appear to. One seat almost always passes, one shares the author's family, all three read a brief the author wrote, and in five cases they read the wrong file. A verdict is best read as a pointer to which findings need checking.

The literature also changes what "independent" should mean for a service like this. Independence does not come mainly from adding vendors, because frontier models increasingly share blind spots. It comes from evidence sources that sit outside the models: content hashes, neutral criteria, test and load-test results, and a human who decides what to apply. The one defect that reached real money came through the panel's own advice and was caught by execution. The next version of Agent Referee should build that pattern in, with panels deciding what to test and execution deciding what ships.
